Over the past two decades, policymakers have increasingly deployed behavioral 'nudges'—interventions in choice architecture that encourage socially optimal behaviors without eliminating options or imposing statutory penalties. Proponents emphasize that nudges preserve individual autonomy while remaining highly cost-effective. Automatic enrollment in retirement pensions exemplifies this dynamic: when citizens are enrolled by default and must actively opt out, participation rises dramatically without fiscal coercion. Similarly, positioning fresh fruit at eye level in educational cafeterias measurably alters nutritional choices at negligible cost. Nevertheless, critics caution against viewing nudges as complete substitutes for legislation. Structural challenges such as industrial carbon emissions, wealth inequality, and severe public health contagions demand enforceable statutory regulations, environmental taxation, and infrastructure investment. Nudges operate effectively as complementary policy instruments, but lack the coercive authority to resolve systemic macroeconomic crises.
